**3. Why the big continents lose their passive edge.** `rift_pairs`: in five of six chord seeds most unbroken-rift pairs
include plate 0, the plate that keeps the supercontinent's core ((0, 54), (0, 42), (0, 35), (0, 27), (0, 37), (0, 39),
(0, 26), ...); new plates p50 2.1 Mkm² (75% under 7 Mkm²). The criterion picks the cut of least strength through a
random nucleus, and the cheapest cut is a short one: from a nucleus near the coast, across a corner. So the big plate
sheds slivers off its rim, again and again, instead of splitting into continents with long conjugate margins; the
slivers' margins are short, many face transforms (cuts not normal to the opening), and the rate law (K, per km of the
candidate's margin) fires short cuts more often. The physics left out: a cut does not break because it is weak; it breaks
when the force it must transmit exceeds its strength (Kusznir & Park 1987, Geol. Soc. Spec. Publ. 28, 35; Buck 1991, JGR
96, 20161, for rifting when the tension exceeds the lithosphere's integrated strength; the forces at plate edges,
Forsyth & Uyeda 1975). A corner cut carries almost no force: the piece beyond it has hardly any boundary pulling it.
Pass 72's design said "the cut through the nucleus that needs the least force breaks first" and then measured only the
strength. That is the defect, and the next arm.

**4. The arm: `rift_line` 'force'.** Same nucleus (random interior continental node), same candidates (cheapest path to
the plate's edge per 5° sector, pairs > 90° apart, ≥ 1 Mkm² on both sides). The cut that breaks is the one nearest
failure: **its opening force over its strength** (the strength is the cut's cost, as 'chord'). The force: the plate
moving as one under its boundary forces and drag, as `force_law` 'torque' books them (its fit to Earth's plates:
`slab_pull_n_m` 5·10¹³ on trench nodes toward the other plate, `slab_suction_frac` 0 on overriding nodes, `ridge_push_n_m`
3·10¹² away from oceanic ridges; basal drag `basal_drag_pa_s_m` with `keel_drag_factor` 3.4 under continent, slab
resistance at `slab_terminal_cm_yr` 8.4); the common rotation ω = M⁻¹τ of the whole plate; the torque the cut must carry,
τ_side − M_side ω (the same from either side); its component about the pole split() opens the cut with (rest centroid ×
side centroid), over R: positive is tension. No new number. Candidates are ranked by that ratio read on the plate's
sectors as seen from the nucleus (nodes sorted by direction, prefix sums: an approximation of each cut's sides, used to
rank only; every candidate costs ~0.4 ms exactly, ~1,300 per draw would be too slow) and checked exactly in that order:
the first that leaves a plate on both sides and is in tension breaks (event `line` 'force'). If none is in tension, the
cheapest cut, as 'chord' (line 'chord'); if no cut qualifies, the 'weak' bisector. Collisions' coupling to neighbours is
left out (assumed).

Found while building: **the starting supercontinent carries no boundary force at all.** It overrides all round, and the
fit sets slab suction to 0; no slab of its own, no oceanic ridge, so every cut on it has zero tension (n = 48 test state:
plate 0, 195 Mkm², 272 overriding nodes, 0 trench, 0 ridge; 40 steps without one). Under the model's own Earth-fit forces
nothing pulls Pangea apart; K's rate law makes the splits anyway. On Earth the drivers proposed are trench retreat
(suction on the overriding plate), mantle upwelling under the insulating supercontinent and its basal traction. The model
has D's traction (`basal_traction`, built, off) and a plate-blind measured field. This is why the arm falls back to the
chord's cut where nothing is in tension (a bisector fallback would mostly bring back the halving line), and it is a
finding for the step-back in pass 74 (item 1's land height and passive edge, item 3's dynamic topography).
